## Abstract

Multimodal change detection usually assumes that SAR and optical images are available at both dates, but the post-change optical image is often lost to cloud cover. We measure what this costs on the OSCD urban change benchmark, using the Sentinel-1 VV rasters released with DS-UNet. Under one shared, deliberately simple training protocol, we compare unimodal U-Nets, early fusion, a synchronous dual-stream CNN, two dual-stream CNNs without the post-change optical image (dropping it or zero-filling it), and two lightweight Transformers (1.2–1.4M parameters), training each configuration with 3–5 random seeds and evaluating on the 10 official test cities. With full information, the dual-stream CNN performs best (F1 = 0.459 ± 0.061, n = 5). Without the post-change optical image, F1 falls to 0.309 ± 0.095 (n = 5); this is still above bitemporal SAR alone (0.241 ± 0.101), but neither gap survives Holm correction at n = 5. Zero-filling and dropping the missing channels perform the same. The cross-attention Transformer reaches unimodal-CNN-level F1 only with strong class reweighting, at a high false-alarm rate, and collapses to predicting no change with an unweighted loss. Seed-to-seed standard deviations of 0.06–0.12 F1 are common: using a single seed, the missing-optical model appeared to beat the synchronous model, the opposite of the five-seed result. A preliminary experiment on a 21-month SAR–optical gap over four SpaceNet 7 sites gives uniformly low F1 (0.08–0.11). We conclude that, at OSCD's scale, losing the post-change optical image costs roughly 0.15 F1, that pre-change optical imagery is at best a modest complement to SAR, and that multi-seed evaluation is necessary for such comparisons to be trusted.

**Keywords:** change detection, SAR–optical fusion, missing modality, cloud cover, dual-stream CNN, Sentinel-1, Sentinel-2, OSCD, seed variance, reproducibility

## 1. Introduction

Urban change detection from satellite imagery is a core task in remote sensing, supporting applications ranging from disaster response and urban planning to infrastructure monitoring. The advent of freely available, short-revisit satellites — particularly the Copernicus Sentinel constellation — has made multi-temporal, multi-sensor imagery accessible at scale. Sentinel-1 (C-band SAR) provides cloud-independent, all-weather coverage with 6-day revisit, while Sentinel-2 (13-band multispectral) offers high-resolution optical data with 5-day revisit.

A persistent challenge in operational change detection is that the two modalities are not always available together. Optical acquisitions are frequently cloud-covered, so in a typical monitoring scenario the most recent usable optical image may be weeks or months old while a post-event SAR acquisition is available. Most multimodal change detection architectures assume both modalities at both dates [1]. When the post-change optical image is missing, a practitioner can fall back to SAR alone, or combine bitemporal SAR with the pre-change optical image. How much performance each option loses, relative to having all four images, is rarely measured.

This work addresses three research questions:

- **RQ1:** How does a dual-stream CNN without the post-change optical image compare with synchronous SAR–optical fusion and with unimodal baselines on OSCD?
- **RQ2:** Can the same models be trained and evaluated on one long (21-month) SAR–optical acquisition gap (SEN12-MT Scenario 2)? We do not study how performance varies with gap length.
- **RQ3:** Are lightweight Transformer models (≈1M parameters) a competitive alternative to the CNNs in this setting?

Because OSCD offers only 10 training cities, we train every configuration with 3–5 random seeds and report seed variability and pairwise tests. We find that losing the post-change optical image costs about 0.15 F1 relative to the synchronous dual-stream model, that adding the pre-change optical image to SAR helps on average but not reliably, and that the tested Transformers depend on strong class reweighting. We also find that single-seed results can reverse the ranking of the leading models; an earlier, single-seed version of this study reached the opposite conclusion for RQ1.

The model architectures are illustrated in Figure 10. The complete F1 comparison across all models is shown in Figure 1, precision-recall trade-offs in Figure 2, and the false alarm rate analysis in Figure 6.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 CNN-Based Change Detection

Convolutional neural networks have dominated satellite change detection since the mid-2010s. FC-EF and FC-Siam architectures [2] established the standard of encoding bitemporal pairs through early or Siamese fusion. U-Net [3] variants remain the most widely adopted backbone due to their encoder-decoder skip connections and strong performance on imbalanced segmentation tasks. Recent work has explored attention-guided Siamese networks [4] and densely connected multi-scale fusion [5]; see [6] for a review. Dual-stream architectures, which encode each modality separately before fusion, are a common design for SAR–optical inputs [1].

### 2.2 Transformer-Based Change Detection

Vision Transformers (ViT) [7] have been adapted for change detection, with architectures such as ChangeFormer [8] and BIT [9] demonstrating competitive performance on optical-only datasets. Token-level attention between temporal branches has been used to model bitemporal correspondence [9]. Lightweight Transformers remain underexplored in the SAR-optical multimodal setting, where patch sizes, channel counts, and dataset scale differ substantially from natural image benchmarks.

### 2.3 SAR-Optical Multimodal Fusion

Multimodal fusion for change detection has been explored through early fusion (channel concatenation), late fusion (decision-level combination), and intermediate (feature-level) fusion strategies [10]. SAR and optical modalities carry complementary information: SAR is sensitive to surface roughness and dielectric properties, while optical imagery captures spectral reflectance. Classical and deep methods are typically developed for a single sensor — SAR [11] or multispectral [12] — and multimodal methods such as [1] assume that both modalities are available at both dates, which does not hold when the post-change optical image is cloud-covered.

### 2.4 OSCD and SEN12-MT Benchmarks

The OSCD dataset [13] provides 24 bitemporal Sentinel-2 image pairs over cities worldwide (Europe, the Americas, Asia and the Middle East) with manually annotated pixel-level urban change masks. OSCD itself is optical-only; Hafner et al. [1] paired each OSCD city with Sentinel-1 VV backscatter acquired near both OSCD dates and released the resulting rasters with their Dual-Stream U-Net (DS-UNet), reporting F1 = 0.600 on the official OSCD test cities. Our SAR inputs are those released rasters (Section 3.2.1). For the multi-temporal experiment we use Sentinel-1/2 data over SpaceNet 7 [14] sites; this corpus is described in Section 3.2.2, and its labels are derived from SpaceNet 7 building footprints, which introduces proxy-label noise.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Problem Formulation

For a scene of $H \times W$ pixels observed at a pre-change date $t_1$ and a post-change date $t_2$, let

- $\mathbf{S}_1, \mathbf{S}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times 1}$ be Sentinel-1 VV backscatter at $t_1$ and $t_2$;
- $\mathbf{O}_1, \mathbf{O}_2 \in [0,1]^{H \times W \times 13}$ be the 13 Sentinel-2 bands at $t_1$ and $t_2$ (reflectance / 10 000, clipped to $[0,1]$);
- $\mathbf{Y} \in \{0,1\}^{H \times W}$ be the reference change mask (1 = change).

A model $f_\theta$ maps its inputs to per-pixel logits $\mathbf{Z} = f_\theta(\cdot) \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W}$, and the predicted change map is $\hat{\mathbf{Y}} = \mathbb{1}[\sigma(\mathbf{Z}) \geq \tau]$ with $\tau = 0.5$.

We compare two input scenarios:

- **Synchronous (full information):** $f_\theta(\mathbf{S}_1, \mathbf{S}_2, \mathbf{O}_1, \mathbf{O}_2)$ — 28 channels.
- **Missing post-change optical ("asynchronous"):** $f_\theta(\mathbf{S}_1, \mathbf{S}_2, \mathbf{O}_1)$ — 15 channels. This models the operational case in which the post-change optical acquisition is unavailable (e.g., cloud cover) and post-change information must come from SAR alone.

The SAR-only baseline $f_\theta(\mathbf{S}_1, \mathbf{S}_2)$ is the natural lower reference for the asynchronous scenario: the question is whether adding the pre-change optical image $\mathbf{O}_1$ to bitemporal SAR improves detection.

### 3.2 Datasets

#### 3.2.1 OSCD

**Optical and labels.** We use the 24 OSCD cities [13] with the official partition: the **10 official test cities** (Brasilia, Montpellier, Norcia, Rio, Saclay-W, Valencia, Dubai, Las Vegas, Milano, Chongqing) are the test set, and the 14 official training cities are split at city level into **10 training** and **4 validation** cities (Beihai, Bercy, Aguasclaras, Paris; fixed draw with seed 42). City-level disjointness of the three sets is asserted in code at the start of every run. Because only 10 of the 14 official training cities are used for training, our numbers are evaluated on the official test cities but are not directly comparable to methods trained on all 14. We use the rectified 13-band Sentinel-2 images (`imgs_*_rect`), divided by 10 000 and clipped to $[0,1]$.

**SAR provenance.** OSCD contains no SAR. Sentinel-1 VV rasters for both dates (`sentinel1_{city}_{orbit}_t{1,2}.tif`, 102 GeoTIFFs covering one or more relative orbits per city) were taken from the public DS-UNet repository of Hafner et al. [1] (GitHub `SebastianHafner/DS_UNet`, commit `8bfa4be`). Following that repository's preprocessing, each SAR raster is resized to the OSCD label grid with bicubic interpolation; no additional radiometric scaling, reprojection or co-registration is applied by us. During training the relative orbit is sampled at random per city; evaluation always uses the first listed orbit. We did not independently verify the Sentinel-1 acquisition dates or the registration accuracy of these rasters; the SAR–optical alignment is therefore inherited from [1]. **Only the VV polarisation is available**; VH is not used anywhere in this study.

**Sampling.** Training draws 64 random 32×32 crops per training city per epoch (640 crops/epoch); no augmentation is applied. Validation and testing run the network once on each full city image (no tiling or overlap). The task is highly imbalanced: 159,077 of 3,077,936 test pixels (5.2%) are labelled change.

#### 3.2.2 SEN12-MT Scenario 2 (Preliminary)

This corpus (referred to here as SEN12-MT; local archive `sen12_mt_urbanmapping`) pairs Sentinel-1/2 time series with 28 SpaceNet 7 [14] sites; it was obtained as a pre-packaged archive whose original publication we could not confirm, and it is distinct from the SEN12MS dataset [15]. For Scenario 2, we construct one training interval: **reference date March 2018 (Sentinel-2) → target date December 2019 (Sentinel-1)**, a 21-calendar-month temporal gap. This reflects realistic operational asynchrony where SAR monitoring begins years after the most recent cloud-free optical acquisition.

We use a **20/4/4 site-level train/validation/test split** (locked via hash). The class imbalance is approximately 93.6:1 (negative:positive pixels). Training uses BCEWithLogitsLoss with pos_weight=94. Labels are XOR-derived binary building-change masks from SpaceNet 7 predictions; they are not independently validated ground truth.

> **Important limitation:** SEN12-MT Scenario 2 results should be interpreted as **preliminary**. The source archive has 85 unresolved flag conflicts in internal metadata; local MD5 checksums were not matched against published hashes; and the semantics of `s2`, `masked`, and bad-S2 index fields remain unverified. Additionally, the test split covers only 4 sites (pooled pixel evaluation); the common-site intersection at 22 untrained months is only 7 sites (5/1/1 train/validation/test), insufficient for a stable multi-gap study. A full temporal sensitivity analysis is beyond the scope of this work.

### 3.3 Model Architectures

All architectures are summarized schematically in **Figure 10** (architecture diagrams for the clean asynchronous CNN and Transformer cross-attention model).

#### 3.3.1 Unimodal Baselines

We train two single-stream U-Nets [3] (base width 64) as unimodal baselines:
- **SAR-only U-Net:** $[\mathbf{S}_1, \mathbf{S}_2]$, 2 channels (VV at both dates); 31.04M parameters.
- **Optical-only U-Net:** $[\mathbf{O}_1, \mathbf{O}_2]$, 26 channels; 31.05M parameters.

#### 3.3.2 Early Fusion

A single U-Net processes the concatenated 28-channel input $[\mathbf{S}_1, \mathbf{S}_2, \mathbf{O}_1, \mathbf{O}_2]$ with no modality separation (31.05M parameters).

#### 3.3.3 Synchronous Dual-Stream CNN

A re-implementation inspired by DS-UNet [1]: a SAR encoder receives $[\mathbf{S}_1, \mathbf{S}_2]$ (2 channels) and a separate optical encoder receives $[\mathbf{O}_1, \mathbf{O}_2]$ (26 channels). The two encoders (four stages, 64→128→256→512 channels, weights not shared) are fused by channel concatenation at every skip level and at the bottleneck, followed by a shared U-Net decoder and a 1×1 output convolution (27.28M parameters). It differs from [1] in loss (BCE instead of a Jaccard-like loss), schedule (50 instead of 390 epochs; lr 1e-4 instead of 5e-5), the absence of flip/rotation augmentation and importance-weighted crop sampling, and fusion at all decoder levels; it is therefore **not** an exact reproduction.

#### 3.3.4 Naive Missing-Optical CNN

The same dual-stream network with the 28-channel synchronous layout, in which the 13 post-change optical channels $\mathbf{O}_2$ are replaced by zeros. It keeps the synchronous channel structure but feeds the optical encoder 13 constant channels that it never saw vary during training.

#### 3.3.5 Clean Missing-Optical CNN ("Clean Async")

The same dual-stream design with the optical encoder input reduced to $\mathbf{O}_1$ (13 channels); the SAR encoder still receives $[\mathbf{S}_1, \mathbf{S}_2]$. Total input 15 channels, 27.28M parameters. The naive and clean variants differ in two ways — the optical encoder's input width (26 vs. 13 channels) and the presence of zero-filled placeholder channels — so their difference cannot be attributed to either factor alone.

#### 3.3.6 Lightweight Transformers

Both Transformer models take the 15-channel missing-optical input. Each modality is embedded with a stride-4 patch embedding (dimension 128) and processed by two pre-LayerNorm self-attention blocks (4 heads, MLP ratio 4, no positional encoding); a convolutional decoder upsamples to a full-resolution logit map.
- **Self-attention variant (1.36M parameters):** concatenates the two encoded streams before decoding.
- **Cross-attention variant (1.16M parameters):** adds one cross-attention block with SAR tokens as queries and optical tokens as keys/values, and decodes the updated SAR stream.

Transformers were trained with BCEWithLogitsLoss(pos_weight=41) in their original configuration (Adam, lr 1e-4, weight decay 0). A small optimizer sweep selected weight decay 1e-4 on validation F1 ("tuned"). A matched-loss variant of the cross-attention model was trained with pos_weight=1 (Section 4.3).

### 3.4 Training Protocol

Unless stated otherwise, all models use:
- **Loss:** BCEWithLogitsLoss, pos_weight=1 (OSCD CNNs); pos_weight=41 (OSCD Transformers); pos_weight=94 (Scenario 2)
- **Optimizer:** Adam (lr=1×10⁻⁴, betas=(0.9, 0.999), no weight decay)
- **Epochs:** 50; the checkpoint with the highest pooled validation F1 (τ=0.5, full validation-city images) is kept and evaluated once on the test cities
- **Batch size / crops:** 64; 32×32 random crops, 64 per city per epoch
- **Data augmentation:** none
- **Determinism:** Python, NumPy and PyTorch seeds set per run; `cudnn.deterministic=True`, `cudnn.benchmark=False`
- **Hardware / framework:** NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060 Laptop GPU (8 GB), PyTorch 2.5.1 + CUDA 12.4

Every OSCD configuration is trained with multiple random seeds (the seed controls weight initialisation, crop sampling and orbit sampling); Table 1 lists the number of seeds per model. We report the mean and the **sample** standard deviation ($n-1$ denominator) over seeds.

### 3.5 Evaluation Metrics

For each model, we report at threshold τ=0.5:
- **F1 score:** harmonic mean of precision and recall; primary metric
- **Precision:** TP / (TP + FP)
- **Recall:** TP / (TP + FN)
- **IoU (Jaccard Index):** TP / (TP + FP + FN)
- **FAR (False Alarm Rate):** FP / (TN + FP); measures specificity

All metrics are computed over pooled pixels across all test cities/sites. Note that pooled metrics over a small number of scenes do not constitute independent site-level sample sizes.

**Full information.** With both modalities at both dates, the synchronous dual-stream CNN has the highest mean F1 (0.459 ± 0.061, n=5) and the lowest seed-to-seed spread among the dual-stream models (**Figure 1**). Early fusion of the same 28 channels into a single U-Net is lower but very stable (0.376 ± 0.022, n=3). Among the unimodal baselines, bitemporal SAR (0.241 ± 0.101) and bitemporal optical (0.204 ± 0.055) are both far below either fusion model; the SAR-only mean is pulled down by two seeds (45, 46) whose selected checkpoints have very high false-alarm rates.

**Missing post-change optical image.** Removing $\mathbf{O}_2$ costs about 0.15 F1: the clean missing-optical CNN reaches 0.309 ± 0.095 (n=5) against 0.459 for the synchronous model. Its precision stays comparable (0.43 vs. 0.42) while recall drops (0.25 vs. 0.52), so the model mostly *misses* changes rather than inventing them, and its FAR (0.023) is among the lowest of the CNNs (**Figure 6**). Compared with SAR alone, adding the pre-change optical image raises the mean F1 by 0.07 and recovers precision (0.43 vs. 0.22), but the seed spread is large enough that this difference is not statistically distinguishable (Section 4.2). Replacing $\mathbf{O}_2$ with zeros (naive variant, 0.297 ± 0.089, n=3) performs the same as dropping it (clean variant).

**Seed variance changes the ranking.** Seed-to-seed SDs of 0.06–0.12 F1 are common, and single runs can collapse (clean missing-optical seed 44: 0.180; SAR-only seed 46: 0.127). Using seed 42 alone — as an earlier version of this study did — ranks the clean missing-optical CNN first (0.412) and the synchronous CNN second (0.387). Averaged over five seeds the order is reversed, and the missing-optical model falls behind early fusion as well. Single-seed comparisons on OSCD-sized training sets are therefore not reliable evidence of a ranking, in line with broader observations on benchmark variance in machine learning [16].

**Transformers.** The original lightweight cross-attention Transformer (1.16M parameters, pos_weight=41) reaches 0.275 ± 0.124 (n=3) — similar to the unimodal CNNs with ~27× fewer parameters — but with high recall (0.63) and very high FAR (0.24). The self-attention variant is lower (0.199 ± 0.048). Optimizer tuning (weight decay 1e-4, selected on validation F1) raised the self-attention model's mean test F1 but lowered the cross-attention model's (Table 1). With pos_weight=1, the cross-attention model fails to learn: in all three seeds the best validation F1 occurs at epochs 1–5, after which the model predicts no change at all (validation F1 undefined), so its reported F1 of 0.088 ± 0.012 (precision 0.05, near the 5.2% base rate) reflects an almost untrained checkpoint (**Figure 12**).

**Validation vs. test.** Validation F1 is often lower than test F1 (e.g., SAR-only seed 42: 0.066 vs. 0.356; **Figures 3–4**). Validation and test use the same evaluation code (Appendix C); the gap reflects the four validation cities, which form a small and atypical sample. Checkpoint selection on such a set is noisy and is one plausible source of the seed variance above.

Per-city F1 varies widely (**Figure 5**): Valencia is near zero for every model, while Montpellier ranges from 0.26 to 0.70 depending on the model. IoU follows the F1 ranking (**Figure 11**); TP/FP/FN counts and qualitative outputs for the seed-42 runs are in **Figures 8–9**.

### 4.4 Comparison with Prior Work

Our test cities are the official OSCD test cities, but our models are trained on 10 of the 14 official training cities (4 are held out for validation), so published numbers are an upper reference rather than a like-for-like comparison. The most relevant reference is DS-UNet [1], which uses the same SAR rasters and reports F1 = 0.600 for its SAR–optical dual-stream model. Our synchronous dual-stream re-implementation reaches a substantially lower mean F1 (Table 1). Plausible contributors are the documented protocol differences (Section 3.3.3: 50 vs. 390 epochs, BCE vs. Jaccard-like loss, no augmentation, no importance sampling, 10 vs. 14 training cities); we did not run ablations to apportion the gap. **All comparisons in this paper are therefore internal comparisons under one shared, deliberately simple protocol, not claims of state-of-the-art performance.** ChangeFormer [8] and BIT [9] are evaluated on very-high-resolution optical benchmarks (LEVIR-CD, WHU-CD) and are not comparable.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 What Does a Missing Post-Change Optical Image Cost? (RQ1)

RQ1 asked whether a dual-stream CNN built for the missing-optical setting outperforms synchronous fusion and unimodal baselines. On OSCD it does not outperform synchronous fusion: when the post-change optical image is available, using it is clearly better (mean F1 0.459 vs. 0.309). This is the expected direction, since the synchronous model sees strictly more information, and the earlier single-seed result that suggested otherwise was an artefact of one favourable initialisation.

The operationally relevant comparison is with SAR-only, because that is the fallback when the post-change optical image is cloud-covered. Adding the pre-change optical image to bitemporal SAR raises mean F1 from 0.241 to 0.309 and roughly doubles precision, but with five seeds per group this gain is not statistically distinguishable from zero. A plausible interpretation — which we did not test directly — is that the pre-change optical image helps the network localise built-up areas where change can occur, while the evidence *that* change occurred must still come from SAR, whose single VV channel at 10–20 m is a weak change signal on its own.

The clean and naive missing-optical constructions perform alike. The earlier conclusion that "channel design is the key decision" was based on single-seed runs and is withdrawn. Since the two variants differ in both encoder input width and the presence of constant placeholder channels, even a difference would not have identified which factor mattered.

**Conclusion on RQ1:** Under our protocol, synchronous SAR–optical fusion is the best configuration, and losing the post-change optical image costs about 0.15 F1. Pre-change optical plus bitemporal SAR is better than SAR alone on average, but not reliably so with five seeds.

### 5.2 Temporal Gap Sensitivity (RQ2)

RQ2 asks only whether the models can be trained and evaluated on one long asynchronous interval (21 months); it does not ask how performance varies with the gap length, and this study cannot answer that broader question. The very low F1 values reflect a difficult task (proxy labels, extreme imbalance, a 4-site test set) rather than a measured effect of the gap. A temporal-gap sensitivity study would require (a) a verified multi-temporal corpus with at least three intervals per site, (b) 15 or more test sites, and (c) multiple seeds per interval.

**Conclusion on RQ2:** Training on a single 21-month interval is feasible, but all models perform poorly, and gap sensitivity is left to future work.

### 5.3 Lightweight Transformers (RQ3)

RQ3 asked whether lightweight Transformers are a competitive alternative. With class reweighting (pos_weight=41), the 1.16M-parameter cross-attention model reaches a mean F1 (0.275) in the range of the unimodal 31M-parameter U-Nets, but below both fusion CNNs, and with a much higher false-alarm rate. With the CNNs' loss (pos_weight=1), it does not learn a usable decision rule: every seed's best validation checkpoint comes from the first five epochs, after which it predicts no change. The matched-loss comparison (Figure 12) therefore shows that this particular model *requires* class reweighting under our protocol. It does not show that CNNs are inherently better than Transformers. The two families still differ in parameter count (~27×), inductive bias, tuning budget and the loss each was tuned for, and only one small Transformer design was tested. We consider capacity and locality bias to be plausible explanations, not established ones.

**Conclusion on RQ3:** The tested lightweight Transformers are not competitive with the dual-stream CNNs under our setup and are sensitive to the loss weighting; broader claims about Transformer architectures would require larger, separately tuned models.

### 5.4 Limitations

1. **Few seeds, low power:** 3–5 seeds per configuration detect only large differences; effects of the size of the pre-change-optical gain (~0.07 F1) would need many more runs. Mean ± SD over seeds describes our runs and is not a confidence interval for the population mean.
2. **Small training set:** models are trained on 10 cities (640 random crops per epoch), without augmentation, and checkpoints are selected on only 4 validation cities. This setting produces the high seed variance we observe and likely depresses absolute performance.
3. **Simplified protocol relative to DS-UNet [1]:** shorter training, BCE loss and no augmentation; absolute F1 is well below the published DS-UNet result, and our conclusions are about relative behaviour under this shared protocol.
4. **Single SAR polarisation with inherited co-registration:** only Sentinel-1 VV is used, and its alignment with the OSCD optical images and labels is inherited from [1] without independent verification.
5. **Transformer comparison:** one small design per attention type; Transformers and CNNs differ in capacity, loss weighting and tuning budget. The matched-loss experiment isolates only the loss weight.
6. **Train/validation split of official training cities:** we train on 10 of the 14 official training cities, so results are not directly comparable to methods trained on all 14.
7. **Scenario 2:** 85 metadata flag conflicts, unverified checksums, unverified flag semantics, proxy labels from SpaceNet 7 footprints, single seed and four test sites; it is preliminary.
8. **Scope and operating point:** urban/building change only; all results use τ=0.5, and other thresholds or class weights would change precision/recall trade-offs.

## 6. Conclusion

We studied SAR–optical urban change detection when the post-change optical image is missing, comparing unimodal, early-fusion, synchronous and missing-optical dual-stream CNNs and two lightweight Transformers on OSCD with 3–5 seeds per configuration. Our findings:

1. **The post-change optical image matters:** losing it lowers mean F1 from 0.459 (synchronous dual-stream) to 0.309; after Holm correction this difference is suggestive rather than significant ($p=0.022$ uncorrected, 0.109 corrected).
2. **Pre-change optical helps SAR on average, but not reliably:** pre-change optical plus bitemporal SAR improves mean F1 over SAR-only by 0.07 and roughly doubles precision; this gain is not statistically distinguishable at n=5.
3. **Dual-stream fusion is the strongest full-information model** (0.459 ± 0.061), ahead of early fusion (0.376 ± 0.022) and well ahead of SAR-only (0.241) and optical-only (0.204).
4. **How the missing image is encoded did not matter:** zero-filling and dropping the missing optical channels give the same F1.
5. **The tested lightweight Transformers depend on loss reweighting:** with pos_weight=41 the cross-attention model reaches unimodal-CNN-level F1 at ~1/27 of the parameters but with high false-alarm rates; with pos_weight=1 it collapses to predicting no change. This is a finding about these models under our protocol, not about Transformers in general.
6. **Single-seed rankings are unreliable at this data scale:** seed-to-seed SDs of 0.06–0.12 F1 reversed the seed-42 ranking of the two leading models, and individual runs of several models collapsed.
7. **Scenario 2 is preliminary:** all models score low on a single 21-month interval with proxy labels and four test sites; it does not address gap sensitivity.

The main contribution is therefore a controlled, multi-seed measurement of how much a missing post-change optical image costs a SAR–optical change detector on OSCD — and a demonstration that single-seed evaluation would have led to the opposite conclusion. Future work should (1) use more seeds and the full 14 training cities; (2) close the gap to DS-UNet's training protocol (longer schedules, augmentation, Jaccard-type losses) to check whether the observed ordering holds at higher absolute performance; (3) add VH polarisation and verify SAR–optical co-registration; (4) train missing-optical models with modality dropout, so that one network serves both the synchronous and the missing-optical case; (5) build a verified multi-interval corpus for gap sensitivity (RQ2); and (6) evaluate larger, separately tuned Transformer models.

## Appendix B: Model Architecture Details

### B.1 Clean Missing-Optical Dual-Stream U-Net

The model processes a 15-channel input tensor:
- channels 0–1: Sentinel-1 VV at $t_1$ and $t_2$ (SAR encoder);
- channels 2–14: the 13 Sentinel-2 bands at $t_1$ (optical encoder).

**Encoders:** two U-Net encoders (not weight-shared) with four stages of 64→128→256→512 channels (DoubleConv blocks, max-pool downsampling).

**Fusion:** SAR and optical features are concatenated along the channel axis at every resolution (128, 256, 512 and 1024 fused channels); there is no separate fusion convolution.

**Decoder and head:** a shared U-Net decoder consumes the fused bottleneck and fused skip features; a 1×1 convolution produces one logit per pixel, thresholded at σ(z) ≥ 0.5.

**Parameter count:** 27,276,609 (synchronous variant: 27,284,097; only the first optical convolution differs).

### B.2 Transformer Architectures

Both models use a stride-4 convolutional patch embedding (dimension 128) per modality, two pre-LayerNorm self-attention blocks per modality (4 heads, MLP ratio 4, no positional encoding) and a convolutional upsampling decoder with BatchNorm to a full-resolution logit map.

**Cross-attention (1,156,993 parameters):** adds one cross-attention block (SAR tokens as queries, optical tokens as keys/values) with residual update and MLP; the updated SAR stream is decoded.

**Self-attention (1,358,081 parameters):** the two encoded streams are concatenated before decoding.

## Appendix C: Reproducibility

All code is in `src/`. Each OSCD configuration is trained for several seeds with

```bash
python run_baseline_multiseed.py --script train_dual_stream_baseline --name dual_stream_baseline --seeds 42 43 44 45 46
```

which archives every run under `experiments/<name>_seed<N>/` (config, per-epoch history, per-city metrics, `metrics.json`) and `checkpoints/<name>_seed<N>/best.pt`, and writes `experiments/<name>_multiseed_summary.json`. Table 1, the pairwise comparisons (Table 3) and `experiments/paper_statistics.json` are generated from those files by `build_results_tables.py`; Figures 1, 5, 6, 11 and 12 are generated from the same files by `generate_paper_figures.py`. No number in the results tables is entered by hand.

**What was checked.**
- *Split integrity:* the train / validation / test city lists are disjoint, and this is asserted in code at the start of every run; the test cities are the 10 official OSCD test cities.
- *Checkpoint selection:* model, checkpoint and threshold choices use validation data only; each test set is evaluated once per run, after training.
- *Metric consistency:* validation and test use the same evaluation function (pooled confusion counts over full-city images, τ=0.5, no masking), so validation–test differences come from the cities, not from the code.
- *Determinism:* re-running the synchronous dual-stream CNN with seed 42 reproduced the originally reported test F1 exactly (0.3868387607). A data-loading optimisation (caching per-city tensors instead of re-converting them for each crop) was introduced before the additional seeds were run; this rerun shows it does not change results.
- *Transformer audit:* re-evaluating the original cross-attention checkpoint gives a test F1 that differs from the saved value by ≈2.6×10⁻⁶ (`experiments/research_finalization_2026-10-15/AUDIT_REPORT.md`); the saved value is reported.

**What was not checked.** We did not independently verify the acquisition dates or co-registration of the Sentinel-1 rasters inherited from [16], and the SEN12-MT archive has the unresolved metadata issues listed in Section 3.2.2. Leakage checks are limited to city-level split disjointness; we did not test for spatial overlap between OSCD cities (which are geographically distinct).

Hardware: NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060 Laptop GPU (8 GB), PyTorch 2.5.1+cu124. A 50-epoch dual-stream CNN run takes about 6–7 minutes on this hardware.
